## Introduction

The Via degli Dei is perhaps Italy’s best-known pilgrimage route after the Via Francigena. It links Bologna to Florence over a total of about 125 km. That is the general picture of the trail. For me, though, the Via degli Dei was my second long walk and, above all, the first one I started on my own. I told myself: “If I walked 250 km to get to Santiago, how hard can 125 km in Italy be?” I had no idea what I was in for.

## The story

The route begins in Bologna, and until the day before I left, temperatures were still summery. So my pack held only an emergency sweatshirt and the usual pair of long trousers I never end up wearing.
I reach Bologna by train and, to my surprise, the temperature has dropped and it is cold. At the tourist office, where I pick up my pilgrim credential, they say it may be even colder on the summit of Monte Adone, so I head for cover: I go to Decathlon and buy a second sweatshirt for a bit of warmth.
After that “emergency” errand, I visit the city; in the evening I watch a light show in Piazza Maggiore, then eat a good mortadella sandwich and go to bed early, because the next day the walking begins.

The first stage is Bologna to Badolo, 29 km. I set out from Piazza Maggiore; I already meet other walkers along the way. The route aims for the Sanctuary of the Madonna di San Luca. To get there I pass under the longest portico in Europe (perhaps in the world). The path climbs, it is still early and there are not many people. I feel the effort, but I make it to the top. Just long enough to see the sanctuary, the view from the hilltop, catch my breath, and then I move on.

The trail drops to Parco della Chiusa and then follows the Reno river. I continue on the flat as far as Sasso Marconi, then tackle the final climb to Badolo. There is almost nothing at Badolo: a handful of houses, an osteria where the food is very good, and that is it. So I spend a quiet evening and recharge. By the end of the day the total elevation is +1,264 m and −784 m. I had expected a pleasant stroll and found a demanding route instead, especially on that final climb.

![San Luca](https://res.cloudinary.com/dx6hrvg6l/image/upload/v1767650892/san-luca_zhariv.jpg)

On the second day comes what may be the hardest stage: the climb to Monte Adone. I leave early and soon find myself inside the woods. The path keeps rising and begins to feel steep. The strain is real, and after a few hours I reach the summit. The view makes up for it: I am in the middle of the Tuscan-Emilian Apennines, and from above you can see hills of green cultivated fields. A postcard landscape. The trail is mountain walking, constant ups and downs with some tough sections. It continues to Monzuno; past that small village I cross a chestnut wood that is truly beautiful, neat and orderly. An image etched in my memory. After that wonderful stretch I reach Madonna dei Fornelli. I am wiped out, exhausted. By evening the distance reads 28 km, with +1,460 m and −1,038 m of elevation. In the evening I eat at B&B Romani and have a wonderful night: the host has put on a dinner especially for Via degli Dei walkers, seating everyone at shared tables, cooking local specialities such as chestnut flour, and telling the story of the area. An unforgettable experience: if you walk the Via degli Dei, you should plan a stop here.

On the third day the kilometres are fewer—“only” 17. You keep grinding through the taxing ups and downs of the Tuscan-Emilian Apennines. I walk the ancient Roman road, deep in the woods yet still well preserved: a piece of history kept for two thousand years.

![Roman road](https://res.cloudinary.com/dx6hrvg6l/image/upload/v1767650895/strada-romana_gohogb.jpg)

I reach the highest point of the route, the Banditacce. Here, tied to a tree, is a bell you are meant to ring to mark the milestone. The forest at this spot is beautiful and clean, and the hard work feels lighter because of the view. From here the path begins to descend. The kilometres pass, I cross the border between Emilia-Romagna and Tuscany and arrive at Passo della Futa. There I visit a moving German war cemetery: the silence is deep, and more than 30,000 German soldiers who died in the Second World War are buried here. Passo della Futa marks the end of the third stage, but there is nowhere to sleep on site. I have to leave the official route to find a bed for the night. I head to Traversa, a tiny place with two restaurants and a B&B, where I spend the night and meet other walkers.

On the fourth day I set out with Monica and Anna, two women from Piedmont I had met earlier and run into again at the B&B. We walk back up toward the German cemetery and, in the fields at dawn, we watch a beautiful kestrel hunting for food.
We carry on and re-enter the woods. The path begins to climb and we reach the Croce di Monte Gazzaro: on top the wind blows hard and the cold morning air makes us shiver. Just long enough to take in the splendid view, snap a few photos, and set off again. Coming down the mountain we pass Passo dell’Osteria Bruciata, a place wrapped in an unsettling legend.
We continue and find ourselves again on a stretch of the old Roman road. At a junction I part from my walking companions: the route splits into a longer variant with more Roman road and the main line toward Sant’Agata. I am too tired to add distance, so I say goodbye and we agree to meet in the evening.
As I walk on I hear engines in the distance: cars and bikes on the Mugello circuit. I am not far from that famous Italian track.
My legs keep eating up the kilometres and I reach Sant’Agata, admire the beautiful church, then continue toward San Piero a Sieve. The way has turned gentle and shifts from dirt to asphalt. By the end of the day my step counter shows 21 km. The hard part of the walk is done: from here on there should be no more serious climbs.

On the fifth day, the penultimate stage, I am walking again with my Piedmont companions. We set off and, following the guidebook, take a detour to visit the Medici fortress. Unfortunately it is closed to the public, so we can only admire it from the outside: high walls with donkeys on top that look like sentries :).
After that we resume the trail and after a few kilometres we are in the middle of olive groves. The view is breathtaking: green, orderly hills terraced with olives. The classic postcard Tuscany.
We admire Castello del Trebbio and right after catch the scent of freshly pressed olives from an oil mill along the route. I would have liked to stop, but their opening hours did not match ours.

![Castello del Trebbio](https://res.cloudinary.com/dx6hrvg6l/image/upload/v1767650885/frantoio_igjfqe.jpg)

We walk on and after a few more kilometres reach the Convent of Monte Senario. After a short break we continue: we are almost there.

The sixth and last stage is always the most bittersweet. The walk is about to end and you replay the past days in your head while you keep moving. I climb the last hill, the one that leads to Vetta le Croci: from here a view opens over Fiesole and Florence from above. A beautiful spot.
I am still with Monica and Anna and we stop for the classic photo on the chairs facing Florence.
We walk on and descend toward Fiesole, visit the town, and stop for schiacciata—a Tuscan flatbread—to refuel. Then we continue toward Florence. The paths through the woods, the hills, the olives, the chestnut groves, and the climbs are already only memories. We are back in the city: traffic, cars, and a fast pace I was no longer used to.
We enter Florence and from the solitude of the Apennines I find myself in crowded streets. We finally reach the destination: Palazzo Vecchio. This walk, too, is finished. I collect my certificate and finally rest.

It was the first trip I took on my own, but as often happens, you are never alone for long. It was a tiring walk that I had completely underestimated and that tested me physically, but the satisfaction of finishing it was enormous.
