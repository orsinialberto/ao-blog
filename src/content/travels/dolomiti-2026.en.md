## Introduction

I've been skiing in the Dolomites for more than 10 years, and I still feel as if I haven't seen it all.  
The Dolomites, a UNESCO World Heritage site, are home to Italy's largest ski area — and one of the biggest in Europe: over **1,200 kilometres of runs**.  
To put that in perspective, it's like strapping on your skis in Milan and skiing all the way to Santa Maria di Leuca, at Italy's southern tip.

That's why every year I try to add something new: a run I haven't done, a different loop, a peak that's a little farther to reach.

Below I describe the routes I managed to ski this year, starting from Corvara and moving around the Sellaronda, pushing out to the farthest linked areas… almost never taking my board off.

---

## From Corvara to the Marmolada

I discovered this loop two years ago; it's become a classic I repeat every time I'm back.  
You leave Corvara following the orange circuit (clockwise), cross Passo Campolongo, and reach Arabba.

Arabba, in my view, is the toughest part of the Sellaronda. It's also where you'll find the famous **Porta Vescovo black run 3**, which — every time I look at it, and especially when I ride it — leaves me breathless.

From the top of Porta Vescovo, the **Marmolada**, queen of the Dolomites, opens up in front of you. At 3,343 metres it's the highest mountain in the whole range.  
Getting there still takes some distance: you loop around Porta Vescovo and follow a long run down to Malga Ciapela. From there a cable car takes you to the summit.

![Marmolada](https://res.cloudinary.com/dx6hrvg6l/image/upload/v1769031242/1-IMG_3678_vrb18z.jpg)

Above 3,300 metres the view is incredible. The Dolomites spread out all around and you find yourself looking around in silence.  
Then comes the descent: an endless-feeling run with red and black sections that test your legs right to the bottom.

To head back you ski toward Arabba on the same runs you took out. From there you can either complete the Sellaronda loop or return toward Corvara via Alta Badia.

---

## From Corvara to Passo Falzarego, 5 Torri, and Cortina d'Ampezzo

The second loop was this year's discovery: from Corvara all the way to **Cortina d'Ampezzo**, via Passo Falzarego and the 5 Torri.

Again you start from Corvara, this time toward Alta Badia, through La Villa and San Cassiano. Here you'll find the **Gran Risa**, one of the most iconic World Cup runs.  
Every time I ski it I reach the bottom wrecked, wondering how anyone can race it at those speeds.

Following signs for Armentarola you reach a car park above San Cassiano. That was the only place I had to take my board off: a shuttle van takes you up to Passo Falzarego.

First, though, I detoured to the **5 Torri**. I hadn't been back in years, and the effect was the same as always: awe.  
Perfect pistes, hardly anyone around, views that make you stop. It's absolutely worth spending time here.

![5 Torri](https://res.cloudinary.com/dx6hrvg6l/image/upload/v1769031248/10-IMG_3568_vmx1zc.jpg)

From the 5 Torri a long gondola runs down to Cortina.  
I hadn't been there in years either, and I was pleasantly surprised: the mountains around are spectacular and the runs are just as good. I would have liked to ski the Olympia delle Tofane, but it was closed for Olympic preparations.

Even with little snow, it was a stop I'd repeat without hesitation.

The return might be even better than the way out. You head back toward the 5 Torri, then onto an isolated stretch deep in the mountains, far from everything.  
A chairlift seems bolted to the rock, and only at the last moment do you see where it's taking you.

Then you're back at Passo Falzarego.

In my personal ranking, Falzarego and **Armentarola** are right behind the Marmolada.  
You climb to Lagazuoi at 2,835 metres, and from there one of the most beautiful runs I've ever skied begins: **7 kilometres** through an incredible landscape.

![Armentarola](https://res.cloudinary.com/dx6hrvg6l/image/upload/v1769031242/4-IMG_3652_ayzprm.jpg)

Canyons, icefalls, huts in the middle of nowhere… it feels like skiing inside a postcard.  
At the end comes the most unusual part: the return. A horse-drawn sled takes you back toward Alta Badia. If you're on skis, you hold a rope and the sled becomes your lift.

An experience that, every year, is worth the trip.

## Sellaronda clockwise and counter-clockwise

A great classic when you start from Corvara is the Sellaronda loop: orange (clockwise) or green (counter-clockwise).

You cross the four passes around the Sella massif:
- Passo Campolongo  
- Passo Pordoi  
- Passo Sella  
- Passo Gardena  

Both directions are spectacular and very scenic. If you have time, it's worth stopping in the different ski areas.

From Passo Gardena, for example, you can branch off to the **Saslong**, one of the most famous World Cup runs: technical, fast, demanding.

On the green loop, passing from Selva toward Canazei, you cross the **City of Stones**: a surreal landscape of huge snow-covered boulders that seem to rise from the ground like a silent city.

---

## Stop in Ortisei

One of the best discoveries this year was ending up — almost by accident — in **Ortisei** and at Seceda.

I leave Corvara toward Passo Gardena, reach Selva, and take the turn for the Saslong.  
At the bottom of the run I notice an unusual tunnel: it's the underground chairlift that goes through the mountain.

I take it, curious where it leads… and I come out on the other side of the massif, with a completely new view of the Sassolungo.

![Sassolungo](https://res.cloudinary.com/dx6hrvg6l/image/upload/v1769031244/15-IMG_3485_ksjlk8.jpg)

I keep going without quite knowing where I'm headed, following the lifts, until I reach the top of a mountain that feels familiar.  
**Seceda**.

The panorama is incredible. From there one of the longest runs I've ever skied begins: **12 km** of pure descent.

An unexpected discovery, and probably one of the highlights of the week.

---

## From Corvara to Val di Fassa

Finally, this year I decided to push even farther. On the map **Val di Fassa** showed up… so why not go see?

I leave Corvara, follow the orange loop to Canazei, then cross the village toward Pozza di Fassa.  
That was the goal, but between timing and distance I decided to stop earlier.

I still reach the Alba area, climb to **Sella Brunech**, then ski back.

This loop was a great discovery too. Not complete, but enough to make me want to go back and explore more.

Because that's the point, really:  
**You don't ski 1,200 kilometres of pistes in one week.**
